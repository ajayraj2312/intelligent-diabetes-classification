Intelligent Diabetes Classification Using Archerfish Optimization and Machine Learning

A web-based clinical screening prototype that combines bio-inspired feature selection with four classical machine-learning classifiers to estimate diabetes risk from eight routine clinical attributes.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus is a chronic metabolic disorder characterised by elevated blood glucose caused by the body's inability to produce or effectively use insulin. Undetected or poorly managed diabetes can lead to cardiovascular disease, kidney damage, neuropathy and vision loss, so early and accurate detection is clinically important.

Machine-learning classifiers such as Support Vector Machines, Random Forests, K-Nearest Neighbours and Naive Bayes can learn risk patterns from clinical attributes. Their performance is limited, however, by redundant, noisy or weakly correlated features. Feature selection removes those attributes, reducing dimensionality and computational overhead.

This project applies Archerfish Optimization (AFO) — a bio-inspired metaheuristic modelled on the hunting behaviour of archerfish — as a wrapper feature selector, then trains and compares four classifiers on the selected subset. A Flask application exposes the full pipeline through a clinical dashboard.

{{ icon('alert', 19) }}

Problem statement

Conventional diabetes classification systems generally use the complete clinical attribute set. This introduces redundant or noisy features, increases computational cost and can reduce classification accuracy. Existing dimensionality-reduction approaches also have limitations:

  • PCA is efficient but may discard low-variance features that carry diagnostic information.
  • PSO and GWO feature selection can converge prematurely to sub-optimal subsets.
  • Feature selection and classifier tuning are often performed separately, limiting the combined gain.
  • Newer bio-inspired algorithms such as AFO remain largely unexplored for diabetes classification.
{{ icon('history', 19) }}

Existing system

Existing studies apply SVM, Random Forest, KNN, Naive Bayes, boosting and quantum classifiers to the PIMA dataset. Comparative studies report strong performance, but most models are trained on all eight attributes without an explicit optimization stage. Where feature selection is used, it is commonly filter-based or relies on established swarm algorithms, and results are rarely integrated into an operational screening interface.

{{ icon('sparkles', 19) }}

Proposed system

The proposed system adds an AFO wrapper stage between preprocessing and classification. The optimized subset is used to train SVM, Random Forest, KNN and Naive Bayes. The best-performing AFO model is persisted and served through a Flask application with a dashboard, patient assessment form, probability result, optimization report, performance analysis, searchable SQLite history and this documentation module.

Archerfish Optimization in this project

Live report {{ icon('arrow-right', 14) }}

Archerfish hunt insects above the water surface by shooting a jet of water. Because light bends at the air–water interface, the fish must correct its aim rather than target the prey's apparent position. School members also compete for the same prey, and the jet's spread determines the chance of a hit.

In this implementation, each archer represents a candidate feature subset encoded as a continuous 8-dimensional position. The school shoots toward the best subset with a refraction-style aim correction, occasionally follows a competing peer, and gradually reduces jet dispersion to shift from exploration to exploitation. Greedy retention keeps only improving shots.

Scope, limitations & disclaimer

  • The PIMA dataset contains 768 female patients of Pima Indian heritage; findings may not generalise to other populations.
  • Only one dataset is used. External or multi-centre validation is outside this phase.
  • Zero-to-missing imputation is a documented heuristic, not a substitute for source-level data quality checks.
  • The models are benchmarked on a single stratified hold-out split; metrics have sampling uncertainty.
  • This is an academic prototype, not a certified medical device. It must not be used for clinical decisions.
